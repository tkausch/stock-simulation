\begin{abstract}
We describe a discrete-event simulation of a single-item inventory with
$(s,S)$ replenishment in which stock is held in lots with individual unit
prices and demand is served from the cheapest lot first.
The mean price of the items currently in stock is tracked after every event.
We explain how this quantity is computed and show that, under cheapest-first
issuing, it is a biased and therefore unusable indicator of purchase prices:
expensive lots are served last, accumulate in the stock, and push the mean
price towards the upper end of the lot-price distribution.
\end{abstract}

\section{Model}

The simulation is implemented in Python with SimPy and consists of three
components.

\paragraph{Stock.}
The stock is a set of lots. Each lot $j$ has a quantity $q_j$ and a single
unit price $p_j$ shared by all of its items. Lots are kept in a min-heap
ordered by $p_j$.

\paragraph{Demand.}
Demand orders arrive as a Poisson process with rate $\lambda$, i.e.\ with
exponentially distributed inter-arrival times. The size of an order is
$\lceil X\rceil$ with $X\sim\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)$, bounded below by~1.
An order is served from the cheapest lot first; when that lot is empty the
next cheapest lot is used. Demand that cannot be served is counted as lost.

\paragraph{Replenishment.}
We use an $(s,S)$ policy with $s=S\,(1-\tau)$, where $\tau$ is the fraction
by which the stock may be empty before a re-order is triggered. After every
demand order, the inventory position (on hand plus on order) is checked. If it
is at or below $s$, an order of size $S-\text{position}$ is placed. It arrives
after an exponentially distributed lead time as \emph{one} lot whose unit
price is drawn from $\mathcal N(\mu_p,\sigma_p^2)$ (truncated at $0.01$).

\section{Computation of the Mean Price}

Let $L(t)$ be the set of lots in stock at time $t$. The number of items and
their total value are
\begin{equation}
  N(t)=\sum_{j\in L(t)} q_j,
  \qquad
  V(t)=\sum_{j\in L(t)} q_j\,p_j ,
\end{equation}
and the mean price per item in stock is
\begin{equation}
  \bar p(t)=\frac{V(t)}{N(t)} .
\end{equation}

The two sums are not recomputed from scratch. They are maintained
incrementally:
\begin{itemize}
  \item when a lot of quantity $q$ and price $p$ arrives:
        $N\leftarrow N+q$ and $V\leftarrow V+q\,p$;
  \item when $k$ items are taken from the lot with the lowest price $p_{\min}$:
        $N\leftarrow N-k$ and $V\leftarrow V-k\,p_{\min}$; if the lot is
        exhausted it is removed from the heap and the next lot is used.
\end{itemize}
After every change of the stock the triple $(t,N,\bar p)$ is appended to a
history, so $\bar p$ is a piecewise-constant function of time. When the stock
is empty, $V$ is reset to zero to remove accumulated floating-point error, and
$\bar p$ is undefined. Demand orders and replenishments, with their lot price,
are logged separately.

\section{Cheapest-First Issuing and the Upward Drift of the Mean Price}

The second panel of Figure~\ref{fig:sim} shows the central observation. Lot
prices are drawn around $\mu_p=10$, yet the mean price of the stock grows to
about $20$ -- more than twice the mean purchase price and even above the mean
lot price plus one standard deviation. This is not a statistical accident but
a structural consequence of the issuing rule.

\paragraph{Mechanism.}
Because demand is always served from the lot with the lowest price, a lot with
price $p_j$ is touched only after all cheaper lots in stock are exhausted.
Under an $(s,S)$ policy the stock is replenished long before it is empty
(here the position never falls below roughly 970 items of 1600), so cheap
lots are consumed continuously while expensive lots are never reached.
Consequently:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item a new lot with a below-average price is consumed almost immediately and
        leaves the stock;
  \item a lot with an above-average price \emph{stays}, and each such lot
        is added to the part of the stock that is never served;
  \item the stock therefore becomes a ``sediment'' of the most expensive
        lots, and $\bar p(t)$ is dominated by them.
\end{enumerate}
The mean price rises during every period between two deliveries, because the
cheapest items are removed and the average of the remaining ones increases.
Each new lot lowers it only temporarily, by the amount that the lot price lies
below $\bar p$, weighted by the lot's share of the stock.

At the end of the run the stock consists of only four lots,

upper tail of the lot-price distribution. In the limit of a long run, a lot is
only consumed when it is the only one left, which the replenishment policy
prevents, so the most expensive lot is in practice never consumed and
the mean price is pulled towards the maximum price.

\section{Consequences}

The mean price of the stock under cheapest-first issuing is a
\emph{survivorship-biased} quantity: it describes the items that nobody
wanted to take, not the items that were bought. It has the following
consequences.

\begin{itemize}
  \item It is \textbf{not an estimator of the purchase price}. Its long-run
        value depends on the number of replenishments and the spread
        $\sigma_p$, not on $\mu_p$.
  \item It is \textbf{not usable as a valuation or cost basis} for the issued
        items, since the issued items are by construction the cheapest ones,
        whereas $\bar p$ is determined by the most expensive ones.
  \item It is \textbf{not a stable state variable}: it trends with simulation
        length and is sensitive to the random seed through single
        expensive lots.
  \item It cannot be used to control re-ordering or pricing decisions, because
        it carries no information about the current market price.
\end{itemize}

For these reasons, the mean price as defined here is useful only as a
diagnostic of this very bias. If a meaningful price statistic is needed, the
issuing rule or the statistic must change. Options are FIFO or LIFO issuing,
a moving-average cost (where issued items leave at the current mean price, so
$V\leftarrow V-k\,\bar p$), or recording the price of the \emph{issued} items
rather than of the items in stock.

\section{Conclusion}

We presented a lot-based inventory simulation with $(s,S)$ replenishment and
cheapest-first issuing, and derived the incrementally maintained mean price
$\bar p=V/N$. The simulation shows that this measure inherits a systematic
upward bias from the issuing rule: expensive lots are never consumed, remain in
stock, and drive the mean price towards the maximum lot price. The problem is
therefore not the mean price itself but the decision to always serve from the
cheapest lot. The resulting property is an artefact of the model and cannot be
used as a price indicator.

\paragraph{Possible fix.}
A natural remedy is to serve demand from both ends of the price range, i.e.\
to take items from the cheapest \emph{and} from the most expensive lot instead
of from the cheapest lot only (for example alternately, or by splitting each
order between the two). Expensive lots would then leave the stock as well, and
the mean price of the stock should move back towards the mean lot price
instead of towards its maximum. We have not implemented or tested this rule
yet; it is the next step of this work.

\paragraph{Limits of the fix.}
Even if the mean price is brought back to the mean, this does not guarantee a
profit. Items taken from the most expensive lots are sold with a low margin,
or at a loss, if the selling price does not follow the purchase price. A
balanced mean price therefore solves the bias of the statistic but opens a
second problem, the profitability of the issuing rule.

\paragraph{Lesson learned.}
The mean price of the stock is not only an output of the simulation. In
reality it influences the selling price and with it the demand. In the model
presented here, demand is independent of the price: the arrival rate $\lambda$
and the order size are constants. A realistic model must therefore close this
loop and describe demand as a function of the (mean) price, for example
$\lambda=\lambda(\bar p)$. Only then can different issuing rules be compared
by their effect on sales volume and profit and not only by the mean price they
produce.
